EthoCrypt

Unsupervised behavior mapping of zombie flies. EthoCrypt films one fruit fly from above and below, sorts every moment into behaviors it discovers on its own, checks that each behavior is real, and compares healthy flies with Entomophthora muscae-infected ones. No keypoints, no hand labels, and no command line for the people running it.

Why it matters beyond flies

Strip away the fruit flies and EthoCrypt solves a familiar data problem: find natural segments in high-dimensional behavior, prove they're real, and put the answer in front of the people who decide.

Discovery without labels

Pretrained vision models describe every frame, and behaviors emerge as clusters on a map. Nobody decides in advance what to count.

Never over-claim a segment

Each cluster is tested across random seeds and held-out animals, checked for confounds like body size, and statistics count each animal once.

Built for the people who use it

A desktop app turns a cluster pipeline into four screens, so an undergraduate can record, upload and run an analysis on day one.

One system, four parts

EthoCrypt started as a cluster pipeline. It is now the whole path from a fly in the rig to a result.

  1. Film

    A mirror rig shows one camera the fly from above and below at once, under infrared light, so recordings run through the night.

  2. Record

    The recorder fixes camera settings, splits the two views, and saves hourly files with a timestamp for every frame.

  3. Send

    The desktop app signs in to the cluster, resumes dropped uploads, starts a run from a lab preset, tracks each fly's progress and archives recordings and results.

  4. Read

    A GPU pipeline ends in one report: what differs between groups, a clip of every behavior, and a verdict on each.

The desktop app

Tracking quality is rated good, check or poor for every fly, with the reasons and the exact frames flagged. Shown here with demo data.

How the analysis works

A foundation model (SAM 2) outlines the fly in every frame. Another (DINOv3) describes each body region, and optical flow measures how the legs and wings move. Those numbers are compressed, laid out on a two-dimensional map, and the dense regions become candidate behaviors.

An optional motion-led setting counts movement three times as much as appearance and subtracts each fly's resting look first, so clusters follow what flies do rather than how individual flies look.

Before anything is reported, each behavior has to reappear with new random seeds and in maps built from different flies. Then the groups are compared fly by fly.

All ten stages
  1. Find the fly: model the empty arena; the fly is what differs
  2. Follow it: SAM 2 tracks a full-body outline
  3. Line it up: crop, scale and rotate every frame
  4. Describe it: DINOv3 per body region, plus optical flow
  5. Compress: PCA, with the variance kept reported
  6. Add time: wavelet rhythms or short frame windows
  7. Map and cluster: UMAP, then dense peaks
  8. Zoom in: re-map one behavior into sub-types
  9. Check it's real: seeds, held-out flies, confounds
  10. Compare: time budgets, bouts and transitions per fly

Credits

Built by Brandon Fricker in the Elya Lab, Harvard University. A standalone, cluster-friendly reimplementation of the CASTLE approach (Liu et al., 2025), with movement encoding after Berman et al. (2014). Licensed GPL-3.0-or-later.

Python 3.11, PyTorch, SAM 2, DINOv3, OpenCV, scikit-learn, umap-learn, pandas, h5py, Slurm on Harvard's FASRC Cannon cluster; a Tauri 2 desktop app with a Rust core; camera control through FLIR's Spinnaker SDK.
