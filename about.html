I'm a postdoctoral fellow in the Elya Lab at Harvard, where I lead four concurrent quantitative projects on how a fungus, Entomophthora muscae, takes over the behavior of the fruit flies it infects. The work runs on computer-vision tracking and noisy datasets of millions of points.

Before that, my PhD at Emory asked what brain circuits drive social decisions in rodents, and as an undergraduate at Lehigh I recorded from pairs of neurons to see how electrical synapses learn.

Across all three, the part I enjoy most is the same: getting from a messy recording to an answer someone can act on, and building the tools that let other people get there too. I'm now taking that into industry, in data science, biostatistics and decision science.

The research behind it

Now: how a parasite takes the wheel

Just before dusk on the day it kills them, E. muscae drives infected flies through a precise routine: they climb, glue their mouthparts down, and raise their wings so spores can spread. Our lab found they also lose their morning activity peak on that last day.

I'm working out which neural and hormonal changes make that takeover possible, from disrupted clock neurons to the circuit that triggers the climb, and built EthoCrypt to find the subtler changes no one has named yet.

PhD: the circuits behind social choices

In prairie voles and Mongolian gerbils I found that pair bonding and partner separation reshape the oxytocin and vasopressin neurons of the hypothalamus.

In spiny mice, a communally breeding rodent, I showed that males prefer larger groups and favor kin, then silenced one cortical circuit to reverse the group preference (read as an A/B test). Along the way I brought the lab's core methods to a species no one had used them in.
