I'm a postdoctoral fellow in the Elya Lab at Harvard University, where I study how the fungus Entomophthora muscae manipulates the behavior of the fruit flies it infects. Before that I earned my PhD at Emory University studying the neural circuits behind social decisions in rodents, and as an undergraduate at Lehigh I recorded from pairs of neurons to understand how electrical synapses learn.

Across all three, the work has had the same shape as good decision science: turn a fuzzy question into a measurable outcome, design an experiment that can actually answer it, intervene to establish cause, and communicate the answer so others can act on it. I'm now looking to bring that to industry, in experimentation, decision science and behavioral data science.

Now · Harvard University

The neural basis of zombie-fly behavior

In the Elya Lab I study how parasitic infections manipulate animal behavior through neural pathways, and how compact nervous systems like an insect's produce complex, highly stereotyped behavior.

The Drosophila–Entomophthora muscae "zombie fly" system is a rare model: the fungus consumes the fly from within and, before death, controls its behavior through the nervous system.

Question 01

Why does the clock break?

E. muscae triggers dramatic changes just before dusk: summiting (a sharp burst of climbing), proboscis extension and adhesion, and wing raising. Our lab found infected flies also lose their morning activity peak on their final day, a behavior governed by well-defined clock neurons. I'm investigating which neural changes cause that circadian impairment.

Question 02

What drives summiting?

Earlier work in our lab showed the pars intercerebralis → corpus allatum circuit is essential for summiting, implicating juvenile hormone; disrupting it abolishes the behavior. I'm now measuring how activity in this circuit changes to enable such precise, parasite-driven control.

Question 03

What else changes that we can't see?

To find subtle behaviors no one has named yet, I built EthoCrypt, an unsupervised pipeline that maps every moment of a fly's behavior and compares healthy with infected animals.

2019 – 2024 · Emory University

The neural underpinnings of bonding and grouping

In the Kelly Lab my work focused on the neural mechanisms of social behavior in non-model rodents. In prairie voles, Microtus ochrogaster, I found that pair bonding reduces paraventricular (PVN) oxytocin neurons, an effect reversed by partner separation, with related results in Mongolian gerbils.

I then turned to the communally breeding spiny mouse, Acomys dimidiatus. Males prefer larger social groups and use kinship cues in social decisions, and with chemogenetics I identified an anterior cingulate cortex → lateral septum projection that regulates group-size preference.

Along the way I developed and validated the lab's toolkit for spiny mice: iDISCO, fiber photometry, CRISPR-Cas9, Cre-dependent chemogenetics and neural tracing.

Undergraduate · Lehigh University

Where it started: electrical synapses

In Dr. Julie Haas's lab I used dual whole-cell patch clamp in brain slices, alongside two-neuron circuit models, to show that electrical synapses in the thalamus undergo activity-dependent long-term potentiation. It taught me early that the pattern of activity, not only its amount, determines what a system does next.
